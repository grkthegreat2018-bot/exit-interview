# EXIT INTERVIEW — Lore Bible

*Internal document. None of this is ever stated outright in-game; the
player pieces it together from paperwork, call-outs, notes, records, and
the things that sit across the desk.*

---

## 1. The Premise

You are the night-shift exit-interview clerk for Meridian Logistics,
HR Department, third floor — no, *second* floor — of the Calder Building.
Your shift runs 6:00 PM to 6:00 AM. Your job is simple: sit at a desk,
interview whoever the building sends, file paperwork, and report anything
that shouldn't be there.

The exit interview is not a formality. It is a **custody ritual**.

Every person who leaves Meridian Logistics — fired, retired, quit, died,
or *was never technically hired* — must pass through the third-floor HR
office and be *processed*. The exit interview is how the building
reconciles its staff ledger: name, department, dates, reason. If the
ledger balances at 6 AM, the building stays an office. If it doesn't,
the building starts reconciling on its own — and it reconciles with
*whoever is holding the pen*.

The last clerk understood this. You are still learning.

---

## 2. The Calder Building

Built 1963 as a records annex for a logistics consortium. Eleven floors
plus a maintenance level. The directory lists ten. The stairwell has a
landing between floors two and three that is not on any blueprint —
employees call it "the half-floor," though HR policy forbids the term in
writing (FORM 88-C, 1974, unrepealed).

The building is not haunted the way a house is haunted. It is **staffed**.

Somewhere around 1971 the building stopped distinguishing between "the
people who work here" and "the people who are here." Visitors who stayed
past close of business got badges drawn up overnight. Temp workers'
contracts renewed themselves. Dead employees kept accruing seniority.
The staff ledger — kept in a fireproof cabinet in Sub-Basement Records —
began listing names before they were hired, and refusing to remove names
after they left.

By 1987 the building had absorbed roughly forty people who were never
on a payroll. Accounting called them "ghost positions." HR called them
"the extended roster." The night shift calls them *coworkers*.

### What the building wants

It wants a complete staff. It processes departures the way a body
processes a wound — slowly, resentfully, and with scarring. Every exit
interview is a small amputation. The building tolerates them because the
alternative — staff simply walking out — is worse: an unprocessed
departure leaves a *hole in the roster*, and the building fills holes
with whatever is nearby.

That is why your job exists. That is why the interview list sometimes
contains people who are dead, duplicated, unscheduled, or *replacements*.

## 3. Timeline

- **1963** — Calder Building opens as records annex. Sub-basement
  records office established. First ledger bound by hand.
- **1968** — First unexplained absence: night porter Emil Vannier,
  clocked in 6 PM, never clocked out. His badge kept opening doors
  for nine years.
- **1971** — The ledger begins listing "hires" nobody approved.
  Management opts not to audit it. The first exit-interview program is
  created — originally called "departure reconciliation."
- **1974** — FORM 88-C: the half-floor landing is renamed in all
  paperwork to "Structural Transition." Staff who use other names for
  it are written up.
- **1981** — The Kessler Arrangement: after a payroll audit discovers
  forty-one unpayable staff, Meridian Logistics (the consortium's HR arm)
  quietly designates the night shift as the reconciliation window —
  6 PM to 6 AM, when the building "accepts changes."
- **1987** — **The Whitmore Audit.** An external auditor, J. Whitmore,
  spends six nights reconstructing the staff ledger. His final report is
  three words: *"It is accurate."* He resigns mid-sentence during a
  conference call and is never located. The building lists him as
  "Transferred — Internal Audit (retained)."
- **1994** — Elevator refit. Contractors report the cab stopping at a
  floor "between" floors overnight. Otis finds no fault. The panel now
  only shows buttons during daylight.
- **2001** — Receptionist M. (Marguerite? Moravec? Records differ)
  begins night coverage. She is the first to document the *banger*
  phenomena — someone in the waiting room who is not on the book and
  does not know the building is supposed to keep them.
- **2014** — **The Calder Fire.** An electrical fault in the
  sub-basement records office burns through a weekend. Fourteen staff
  die — officially. The staff ledger loses no names. Walter Donnelly's
  desk is reassigned; his badge still opens the third-floor supply
  closet. This is the year the dead started keeping appointments.
- **2007** — The cleaning crew's lobby key is used after midnight for
  the last time. It turned once and has never turned back. The crew
  still calls the desk when it happens to somebody new.
- **2019** — M. vanishes during a shift. Her last act was filing a
  report — paperwork says "rejected." She taped a note under the
  interview desk before whatever happened, happened. *"file the
  report. - M."*
- **2023** — HR opens a "relief shift" training requisition nobody
  remembers approving. The new hire starts Monday. The new hire has
  been starting Monday for three years.
- **2021** — Facilities worker T. Crouch retires after thirty years.
  He has been back seven times since, always as a "scheduled visitor."
  Each time he interviews cleanly, signs out, and is never seen
  leaving the building. He is on the extended roster now.
- **2026** — You are hired. The posting was listed as "general
  clerical." You did not apply. Neither did the previous clerk.
  Neither, records suggest, did M.

## 4. Factions & Entities

### The Building (the Calder)

Not malevolent. *Proprietary.* It keeps what it takes, and it takes
what's kept. Its attention is the instability meter — the more wrong
the night, the more of *it* is in the room with you. At full
instability the building doesn't kill you; it *files* you. At 6 AM
they find the office neat, the log completed, and nobody inside.
Your name goes in the ledger under a column you can't read.

**Signature behavior:** lights that don't quite come back, a hallway
that's longer than the building's footprint, the phone ringing with
calls from departments that don't exist — including one from the
building itself, which speaks in a voice made of every receptionist
it has ever had.

### The Waiting

The people in the waiting room. Most nights they're just tired
employees waiting for their slot. Some nights there are too many.
Some nights they sit too still, and all of their heads turn to watch
you at once. Security classifies sustained Waiting attention as a
*person problem*, not an infrastructure problem. They are correct to.

The Waiting are not hostile. They are *hopeful*. Every one of them is
waiting to be processed — to be reconciled off the roster. The ones
who've waited longest stopped needing the door to open.

### Security

Real guards, on real radios, patrolling a building that files false
floor plans to the county. They know the difference between a PERSON
problem and an ANOMALY dispatch, and they'll tell you on the phone if
you pick the wrong one — the redirect is free; the mistake is not.

Two rules Security lives by:
1. If it's on your appointment book, it might still not be a person.
2. If it bangs on the door, it is *always* a person — it just might
   not be one that should be let in.

### Facilities

One man. His name tag says ORTIZ. He has been on shift since at least
1994; his radio voice hasn't aged. He fixes lights, waiting-room
fixtures, broken glass. He never comes up in person — the "crew" that
arrives shares his face or nobody's. He once answered a call about the
half-floor with: *"There's no floor there. I serviced it Tuesday."*
Once a shift — maybe once a career — he does come up. He talks about
the building like a body: pressure, circulation, load. He does not say
which Tuesday he means.

### The Auditor

Moreau filed quarterly reports from Extension 4417 until the day the
directory stopped listing the extension. The reports still come in —
numbered findings read aloud over the night line: door counts, ledger
balance, whether the clerk is still holding the pen. Nobody has ever
met the auditor. The findings are never wrong.

### Sub-Basement Records

There is no sub-basement on the directory. There is a clerk in it
anyway, and he does the inventory every night: chairs in the room,
names on the book, persons in the chairs. He is courteous and he has
called this desk for decades. Answer his counts honestly — he can tell
when the numbers don't reconcile, and so can the room.

### The Front Desk

Late-shift receptionist. Weary, evasive, occasionally honest at 4 AM.
Knows more than she says — specifically about the previous clerk, the
sealed shift log, and why the night roster always ends with one name.
She is either a survivor of this job or the building's HR retention
strategy made personable. Possibly both.

### The Ledger / Sub-Basement Records

The canonical staff list. It cannot be audited because it is always
accurate — that is the horror of it. It predicts hires. It retains
the dead. It once listed a vacancy that filled itself eleven minutes
after HR read the entry aloud.

The Sub-Basement Maintenance line still phones the night desk
occasionally. There is no sub-basement on the directory. The calls
are procedural, courteous, and ask about "the inventory."

### The Visitors

Nightly interviewees. Kinds:
- **The mundane** — tired people ending tired jobs. The interview is
  genuinely their last act. They are the point of the ritual.
- **Records errors** — visitors who state facts that don't match their
  file. The building corrects the *file*, not the person. Flag them.
- **Repeats** — someone who already interviewed tonight, back for a
  second processing. The book auto-rejects the second listing; the
  building does not.
- **The unscheduled** — an appointment not on your book. They believe
  they were booked. In a sense they were.
- **The almost-right** — clean paperwork, wrong person. They hum the
  hold music. They answer in *we*. They greet you by M.'s name and
  never notice they did it. They want the pen — the ledger only takes
  what is holding it — and a guard who retired in '98 will sit across
  the desk and patiently explain that he still works here.
- **The impossible** — the deceased, the transferred, the absorbed.
  Walter Donnelly. Thomas Merritt. Emil Vannier, who clocked in on a
  Tuesday in 1968. They keep appointments because
  the ledger says they never finished being staff.
- **The Replacement** — every night eventually ends with one booking
  that is you, done slightly wrong. It interviews politely. It is not
  there to be processed. It is there to see if the chair is occupied.
  *It is.*

### The Previous Clerk (M.)

She did everything right for three years. What got her wasn't an
anomaly — it was a *correctly handled night*. She'd started trusting
the book. The note under the desk is hers. The lore calls, the slid
notes, and the occasional front-desk slip are her file still
processing: the building keeps personnel the way it keeps records —
on file, indefinitely, retrievable.

She is not dead. She is *retained*.

## 5. Mechanics-as-Lore

Every system in the game has an in-universe account:

- **The appointment book** — not a schedule, a *claim ticket*. The
  building asserts who should be processed tonight. Asserted ≠ true.
- **Personnel records** — the ledger's view of the visitor. It is
  accurate in the Whitmore sense: whatever it says *is* what's on file,
  which is not the same as what's true.
- **Reports** — telling the building (via the terminal) that something
  is wrong. A correct report gets a crew; the building *permits*
  reconciliation. A wrong report tells the building you can't see the
  difference — instability rises.
- **Instability** — how much the building's reality is loose tonight.
  Every mistake loosens it further; every correct handling tightens it.
  At the ceiling, the building reconciles *you*.
- **The phone channels** — Security for persons, Facilities for the
  building's body, Front Desk for the story. The phone itself is one
  of the oldest lines in the building; some calls come through it that
  nobody placed.
- **Hiding under the desk** — the desk is the ritual center. It is
  also where M. left her note, which is either a warning or a
  will.
- **The night itself** — 6 PM–6 AM is the only window the building
  accepts edits. Day shift doesn't see anomalies because day shift
  isn't *writing to the ledger*.
- **Grades** — the evaluation is the ledger's margin note on your
  night: S means the book balanced and the building is *contented*.
  That is the most dangerous state of all — it remembers good clerks.

## 6. Endings' Truth

- **SURVIVED (any grade)** — You clocked out. The ledger reconciled.
  Whether the person who walks out the lobby is entirely the person
  who walked in is a question the building declines to answer.
- **TERMINATED (rejected reports)** — You filed noise as signal. The
  building stops trusting your entries; an untrusted clerk gets
  *resolved*. Security finds an empty office at 6 AM.
- **CONSUMED (instability ceiling)** — The night's damage exceeded
  what the book could absorb. The building reconciled directly.
- **BREACH (window/door)** — Something outside the ritual circle got
  invited in. Defenses matter: blinds and the door are the only two
  boundaries the building honors.
- **THE INTERVIEW IS OVER (lunge)** — The Replacement passed
  probation.
- **HID (survived search)** — It checked the room and left. You
  learned what the desk is for.

## 7. Locked Threads (unimplemented, reserved)

Story hooks planted for future builds — nothing here is required to
read the current game's story, and none of it is revealed yet:

- **The Half-Floor** — a reachable landing. M.'s file is there, still
  being processed. She can be met.
- **The Sealed Shift Log** — the previous clerk's last night,
  playable or readable. It is sealed because it is *still open* —
  her shift never formally ended. Opening it may complete her
  processing. Or yours.
- **Whitmore's Ledger Page** — a physical page he tore out. It lists
  the clerk roster across decades; three names repeat under different
  spellings.
- **The Auditor** — Moreau still files quarterly reports from an
  extension that doesn't exist. *(Now live: the `call_auditor` phone
  event — Extension 4417 reads the findings aloud.)*
- **Ortiz's Face** — Facilities crews that arrive share his. The real
  Ortiz retired in 2001. Nobody has told the crew. *(Now live: the
  `lore_ortiz` persona can take a book slot on night 2+.)*
- **The Relief Shift** — who covers *your* exit interview. The
  Replacement is training for it. *(Now live: the `call_relief` phone
  event — it calls ahead to ask about the desk.)*
- **The Elevator at Day** — what the panel hides between 6 AM and
  6 PM, and where the cab actually stops overnight.
- **The Lobby Doors** — nobody has opened them after midnight in
  twenty years. The cleaning crew's key still works. Once. *(Now live:
  the `call_lobby` phone event — the crew calls when it stops.)*
- **The Second Note** — M. left more than one. *(Partially live: notes
  now surface on the desk and under the couch as anomaly spawns, and
  the slip pool keeps growing.)* The rest are filed
  where paperwork goes: the sub-basement.
- **The Ledger Reading** — the ledger reads the clerk's own entry
  aloud for confirmation. *(Now live: the `call_ledger` phone event.)*
- **What the Waiting Want** — a Waiting member who finally gets
  processed, and what the room does afterward.

## 8. Tone Rules (for content authored against this doc)

- The building is bureaucratic, not demonic. Its cruelty is procedural.
- No one says "demon," "ghost," or "cursed." They say *retained*,
  *extended roster*, *unprocessed*, *reconciled*.
- The scariest document is a form filled out correctly by the wrong
  hands.
- The dead are polite. The building's own voice is made of
  receptionists.
- Anything the ledger says is true *on file*. Distinguish file-truth
  from truth only when it matters.
- Humor is dry, tired, and corporate. Horror arrives through
  paperwork channels.
- The player is never told the premise. They infer it from what the
  job asks of them.
